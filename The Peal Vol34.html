Letter from the Editors

Welcome to the 2024-2025 edition of The Peal. This semester has been full of grief as we have all faced damage from Hurricane Helene. With its mighty winds, Hurricane Helene blew through Swannanoa, but that did not stop the creative minds at Warren Wilson. Despite the instability, we, the editors of the Peal, have worked hard o assemble this collection.

This edition of The Peal has a musical theme, the first of its kind. Music has a way of seeping into everything we do, from the soft notes of a piano to the rhythmic flutters of turning a page in a book. This collection showcases how we interact with music and how it affects us

A huge thank you to everyone who submitted pieces and to anyone who has created anything during these troubling times. this is only a fraction of the incredible things the Warren Wilson College communiity creates and we love seeing every single piece.

Sincerely,
The Peal Editors

Helene's Whisper

Bella Sosa

When Helene roared from the coast,
I felt the air tighten,
heard her breath howl through every hollow bone.
She came like a hand across the earth,
turning rivers into monsters,
and my mother never left.

She was supposed to return after Family Weekend,
but the sky bent her path into silence,
a signal snuffed out by storm winds as she drove deeper into the eye.
No word from her.
Only the blank stare of my phone as if the world had folded in on itself,
leaving me hollow.

When news came,
it was of her in an attic,
cans of beans,
peanut butter crackers,
water licking at the walls like a hungry mouth.
She'd climbed above the flood,
but no one could tell me where the water would end.

She had found a payphone on a mountain-
her voice faint, like something pulled
from the edges of a dream-
and said, "He might come get us. I don't know.
This is all I have for now."
Static filled the gaps where I wanted to ask if she was ok.

Three days without power,
three days without the sound of running water,
and when she arrived mud caked and tired-
She told me to pack up and leave,
I couldn't understand it,

the abruptness of her words,
as if the storm had shifted something inside her,
and now everything had to be abandoned.
But I couldn't.
My life had tangled itself in this place,
its people were rooted in my chest.
"I need time," I said,
and she promised nothing.

The next day, she tried to return,
hitchhiking back through broken roads,
but the route had vanished beneath her feet,
and instead,
she was swallowed by Asheville's airport,
leaving me to find my own escape.

I remember the journey-
the bodies pulled from floodwaters,
the earth torn apart by winds stronger than anything I had ever known.
Uprooted trees leaned against the sky like crooked hands trying to hold it back.

When I got home, I couldn't eat.
Food tasted like damp wood, like grief.
Water ran through faucets, and it was a knife,
its sound unraveling me, as if the storm had left its voice
in the pipes beneath our house.

Now, even in quiet rooms,
I can hear Helene in the walls.
The floods never really recede.
They only find new places to rise.

Grew up hard, fell off harder

Anna Sauerbrey

There is a clarinet in my childhood bedroom.
It lives in a nice brown case, lined with old,
Crusty velvet saturated with spit. Somehow
There is no association to middle school band,
To the glob of wax pressed over my braces
So I didn’t cut my lower lip, my phone hidden
Behind a music stand and open to a game,
Scales performed clumsily on a chipped reed,
Forged practice logs, the cold vibration
In my chest before we went on stage, my dress
Too thick and warm around my arms
But my legs were so cold they tinged blue,
Unable to shake the feeling.
I do not look at the instrument.
It is shoved in the bottom of a cabinet
In the corner of the room, out of sight
And waiting for some impossible future.

House of Straw

Rowan Ts'eh Berry-Foster

It snowed nearly every day I saw Conchobhar. The air was thick with pre-precipitation, great webs of ice from tree to tree. I was newly lonely, stranded for a month on my mother’s farm, a half-hour to the clos est town. He lived down the road. I was tired of waiting for my girlfriend to notice my self-defying acts of martyrdom. I fed him undercooked chick en and he brought me terrible boxed wine. I bribed my little brother with the PlayStation to stay in his room, and I didn’t tell my parents, who were off on a romantic, productive trip for my mother’s work.

I took off my shirt before he reached for it. He was the third man I’d slept with, and I was convinced the rigidness of men’s bodies, the smell of their sweat, the universal cowlick at the back of their heads was nothing to gawk at anymore. I considered myself worldly, able to slip in and out of bed without creaking the springs, able to ignore the bust of a dictator in his bedroom, able to trail down the stairs and drink a beer with his mother on the sofa.

Before we met, he told me he would be leaving for Colombia in a month’s time. It was, in part, to guilt me. I’d stopped responding to his messages out of the blue, months ago, and he had bought a ticket in retri bution, to prove he needed no one. I faced this timeline with grim deter mination. If something was to be taken from me, knowing would alleviate my helplessness, surely.

We watched my poetry lectures in my bed, heads swiveling to double, triple-check that my laptop was still muted. He recounted stories of ladies he’d slept with when using a Couch-Surfing app in Taiwan, men who’d taken him by the arm in bars, and the two women he’d loved: a blonde, french woman whom he’d left behind when he came back to the states, and a fellow tour guide in Alaska.

When he wasn’t rubbing my back he was telling me stories that seemed at best dubiously true. Getting so drunk he could only speak Span ish, a Craigslist ad posted by a couple that seemed suspiciously like they were going to kidnap him, an older woman who’d tried to persuade him and his friend to let her touch them while they slept.

He told me the most beautiful part of my body was my shoulders. My parents loved him. I got terribly drunk and ruined a game of spades with my family one night, and he put me to bed like I was a small, disgruntled child. This too I liked, the five years that lay between us.

She’d never liked kissing. She took his hand and put it low, low on her stomach. Held it there. The room was dark. “This,” she said, “is my favorite part.”

HOUSE OF STICKS

Conchobhar left for Colombia. My mother urged me to stay in touch with him. We texted some, and then we didn’t. One night in late January, I called him from outside a friend’s apartment, drunk and love sick, eager to dip my head into the Lethe and forget everything but his hold on my ankle. He didn’t pick up.

𓃦𓃥

After my parents spent that first night together, my mother’s then-husband forbade her from seeing my father. He showed up furious at my father’s door. He threatened to bring claims of infidelity into the court case, though mom had known for years that his clients sucked him off under his desk.

Months go by. My father is riding his bike to work when he sees my mother driving. He runs over to the window, breathless. Her hair is cropped to her neck. “I was bringing this to your house, just now,” she gasps. She hands him her shorn hair through the car window.

HOUSE OF BRICK

I slept with other people. When Conchobhar messaged me, I ex tracted only enough personality to keep him interested and injected it into late one sentence replies. He came home for his birthday, so he stopped by my dorm. He wore a baseball cap, which repulsed me. I worried my friends would see him enter my room, so I rushed him from the side door quickly, hardly looking at him.

He brought me fried catfish, which sat limply in its styrofoam cas ket as I told him I was no longer interested. He cried, and I felt relieved at the sogginess of it. There was no beauty to latch onto.

“What did you think would happen?”

He wiped a string of snot on his sleeve. “I don’t know, I just thought that I would come up here and like, charm you? That I would be charming? And you’d invite me in…”

He trailed off, looking at the wine bottle sticking out of his bag. I sat on my bed, poised.

“And then what?” I asked, “We would have sex? And then...” He raked his hand through his hair and looked around the room. “Yeah, probably. I thought, and I know it’s stupid now–,” his voice trembled, “...that I would stay a few nights, that you would want me to.”

“And then you would leave again.”

He nodded. He had the decency to look ashamed.

When he leaned to kiss me at the door I looked straight down at his shoes, and his lips crushed wetly against my forehead.

𓃦𓃥

On Conchobhar’s 26th birthday, I texted him on Facebook, just to hold the words in my mouth, to click on his name. We talked about dogs and made allusions to our romantic entanglements. He was in Mongolia, attempting to “coax a house dog to drop her collar.” This meant he was trying to seduce a woman out of a relationship and into his bed. It prob ably worked. He sent two songs about me he’d written, and both of them were beautiful and mean. I didn’t tell him I wanted to taste his mouth again. I said happy birthday. It was snowing where he was. I closed my eyes and imagined it covering me. Only the tip of my boots peeked over the snow. Conchobar made his way from the house to me. He scraped the snow from my head, and I pretended to be asleep. “Dead! They’re dead!” he howled. He pinched my cheek as I kept my face slack.

I opened my eyes a sliver and saw him rolling a snowball. I bolted upright, tackled him into the snow, gloves wet and freezing as I slipped them under his scarf. He squealed for mercy.

HOUSE OF EARTH

Radio Silence. The following summer, against my better judgment, I looked Conchobar up on the Couchsurfing app. Still in Mongolia. Some one who stayed with him wrote it was like staying with their own brother. I burst into tears at the food pantry, holding cases of great value mac and cheese. I bit my nails into nothing at the office, staring at his profile pictures. His hair had grown to his shoulders, and he wore it tied back, like my dad does, like my partner does.

𓃦𓃥

In November, Mom calls and says she and my dad are separating. Two weeks later, when I ask if that is in fact, happening, she says, “Not today.” So I delete the playlist I’d made about them. No more sitting on my living room couch and sobbing to Fiona Apple’s “Across the Universe” Music Video.

Instead, I watch videos Conchobhar sent me from his childhood bedroom, from Colombia, from Alaska, from Mongolia. In my favorite he’s outside a hostel, socked feet on brick, phone propped on a plastic chair. He almost sings my name, but stops himself. The song could be about any pretty girl. I feel safe in this obscuration.

He’s coming back home this December, and though I’ve told him I would like to see him, I know I won’t go through with it. I am nothing if not oppositional.

I think about the last time I saw him, in his truck, the window rolled down to look at me waving. On the way to Charlotte to fly to Co lumbia, already there mentally, the sun clear in his eyes. His cheeks were pink. His nose was red. He was no wolf.

Kits

Maddy Clements

In a hidden alleyway, two children in a small crowd clap for a nearby musician.

Hush!

Do you hear the toll bells, ringing
across the valley far?
Do you have a coin to spare, or two
to make your entrance sure.

Did you see the tuft of ear from the
grey rabbit on the bridge?
You can’t get too close to pearly
teeth, or you will force its hand.

Don’t you look under the stones,
If hunger drives you raving
The rabbit beat you to the meal
hollow, fearful, manic,
desperate, unfed, craving…
until

the young will not be seen again.
buried in ground, then buried in gut,
the downpour of rain washing away
the crime of the Elder.

Hearken, children, for you are the Kits
the little ones under the ground.
Take your hands, dig up and out
before your hearts take the hit.

Strike a flame, flip a coin
into the Well instead of bells.
Bite away at the chains that bind you,
With your rabbit teeth
they’re sharp as Hell.

A few seconds later, a pair of armed guards appear and haul the musician away by the elbows. The crowd claps heartily. For whom, it is unclear.

The Glory of Innocence

Anna Sauerbrey

When I first met you, you were screaming
for your mother even though she was right there.
Her paleness must have been hard to spot
amongst the grass, your eyes bleary from birth.
The two of you sat beneath the bush and you cried
and you were the most perfect thing I’ve ever seen.
Your coat pristine, not yet stained with the thick
clay soil that will dye your hooves russet.
Your mother is deformed, but not you, not yet.
With little regard for fragility or fear I held you,
so new you didn’t understand danger, only
looked at me with those eyes, legs too weak
to hold you up. I tried to teach you to be mean,
to demand from her what you needed. Your breath
smelled like nothing at all.
What I wanted for you was colostrum,
fresh from your mother, who was crying too.
Not because I touched you,
but because you had not drunk.

Snowy Footprints

Trevor Nail

I felt in my hands the frizzled strands of the end of her hair. Although this was the part of her hair that had been dyed years ago, dam aged beyond repair, it was still soft enough to satisfy my needs. I turn over in her bed, which is more comforting than any piece of furniture I’ve ever laid on, and I look out the window directly next to me. The light of day has just creeped over the horizon and I see below that there is snow on the ground. We suspected there might be snow, but after getting carried away last night, we completely forgot that I may be stuck here all day. I lay back down and reach gently over her soft sleeping body for the television remote. I turn it on and put on some miscellaneous gentle piano music, knowing I want stimulation, but that I also don’t want to wake her.

As the piano chords begin to create a fragile, yet undeniable energy in the room, she shifts in her sleep, rolling away from me. As she settles into a more comfortable position, the blanket falls off her back, exposing the pale canvas of her skin. I reach over with my fingertips, as if they’re paintbrushes, and begin to draw circles on her back. Every downstroke was in time with the screen’s music. The melody elicits the same kind of feeling I have when I touch her skin. It’s a warmth I can’t quite reach. I felt as if I was experiencing something important to life itself here. This must be one of those things that causes human beings to fight for life. After a couple minutes of this, I move my hand away, and she impulsively reaches her small hand in the air and waves for two strokes before letting unconsciousness take it back down. She’s either subconsciously communicating with me or she’s been awake this whole time, just enjoying affection. So I keep touching her back because, regardless, it’s what she wants.

I lean in closer to her and I catch a whiff of her hair. I scoot closer yet again, just close enough that the edge of my nose can reach the fullness of her blonde waves. I close my eyes and I’m transported to the womb. I am nothing but a child, holding onto the warmth and sensations of femininity. Feeling Oedipal in thought, I pull myself away from her to examine something other than her beauty. I don’t want to look into a screen, and so I stand up and look at myself in the mirror. Naked, sad looking, and, honestly, strange, I wonder how I could be welcomed into such a special place that has been curated and looked after by a person filled with the divine feminine. The piano stops; the end of the first song. The one that starts up now is somber; emotions can be felt easier through this kind of sound.

I look at the shape of my body, my muscular legs, my strange torso, my scraggly facial hair, and my bulging Adam’s apple. I realized at this moment that men are ugly. Maybe not all men, but I certainly am. Perhaps we’ve gotten it all wrong. What is a (straight) man’s life for if not to admire the feminine, the beauty that we cannot be? I lay down on her bedroom floor and looked at the ceiling fan. As it spins, I began to wish that I had been born into a woman’s body. Masculinity isn’t all it’s made out to be, and if anything, it is just a tool. A tool for what?

It is at this moment that I stand up quickly, still trying to avoid making large or abrupt sounds, and I begin to put on my clothes. Once I’m layered up, I walk down the stairs into her kitchen. I begin to make coffee, and while the water boils, I search for a shovel for the snow. I find one on her back porch, and I turn around to the open door and see her little bea gle sitting at the edge of the door, waiting for my attention. I walk over to her and kneel down, noticing her paw dangling in the air, a sign of sadness that can only be treated by gentle love.

“Hey, Lila-Girl,” I say as I kneel down to pet her. After a moment, I realize another reason she may be standing here.

“Do you want to come use the bathroom, or are you afraid of the cold snow?”

She just stares innocently up at me. She doesn’t want to be cold. “Okay, Lila, well, you gotta use the restroom somehow.”

So I stand back and pick up the shovel. I walk off the porch and into the yard where I begin to clear out an open space in the grass. I walk back to Lila and I pick her up. She squirms a little at first, probably due to her needing to pee, but then I place her in the freshly made clearing. She immediately begins to pee, and, upon finishing, turns all around to see if there’s a way to move that doesn’t involve walking in the snow. There isn’t, so I just pick her up again and walk her back inside.

By the time we got back in, the water had been boiled and I made two cups of coffee. I walked them carefully upstairs and then entered her room again. She was still asleep, but the sound of the door opening made her reveal her deep blue eyes to me. In a flash of consciousness, she realized that I had brought her coffee, and she closed her eyes again, but whispered a subtle and liberating “Thank you.” I smiled and placed the mug on her side table. The music still created a blanket of atmosphere that hung over the room like a warm and dull lamp. I drank my coffee as quickly as possible, then walked downstairs and out the front door. I began shoveling snow as efficiently as I could, making a path for her to safely get to her car and drive away should she need to. I figured that perhaps this is what makes being a man a good thing. As I labored, I tapped into what must have been a deeply historical motivation; with every exhausting upward stroke of the shovel, I would look back and up into the window of her room.

Our House

Andaluna Malki

When they arrive at the sliding glass door, absolutely everything begins to look, well, a bit futile. Their fingers lick at the panes. The nails that never stopped growing itch at the glass. We had it reinstalled about seven months before the Event took center stage. I thank someone for the timing. I mourn the loyalty the door had in my life. With her never replaced hinges and wheels, nearly 19 years of dog nose smears, and muddy handprints on its history, how could I not?

The sky is a clear blue behind the decaying heads of about 12-17 Lame Brains. Zombies. I could’ve used Roamers like one on the show, but it made me too creeped out. Lame Brains sounds like a mob from an over ly-saturated flash game. They moan and groan the most annoying tunes. I wish they could at least have some harmony.

I grab a stool and cover the windows with sheets. I never realized how many fitted sheets we had until I was stapling a fifth one to the bot tom of another’s curving elastic. Before I finish nailing the last corner to the floor, I grab a bag of rambutans.

The space between the Lame Brains is 5 inches. 2 of those are glass. I know - hefty door.

I look up to them. Most don’t even notice. About three from the side limp towards me.

I dig my thumb nail into the red spiny skin. I break it open to reveal the white fleshy fruit inside. I pop the morsel, pit and all, into my mouth. I chew over eagerly, quietly growling back. I use my fingers to pick the flesh out of my teeth, then feed it back in. The wooden outer crust of the pit sticks to some of the fruit’s chunks. I don’t bother to take those pieces out.

After I spit the pit, sucked and bit clean of fruit, into my hand, I do another round of staples around the sheets. I hang a curtain of cans filled with coins and rocks. I never bother looking at those poor souls again until I drag their unmoving carcasses to my monthly burn pile.

It started slower than you would think. It’s like the parasites- no, maybe it’s a virus. I don’t remember. It’s like it wanted to watch us scramble for plans A-Z and vitamin C.

My mom and I wanted to stay home. That was how we were going to get through this. We already had a fenced-in property. We reinforced it with steel rods, cut into spikes. We set noise traps in the small forest outlining our land.

I let go of Mom about two weeks ago. She got sick. Flu, something else - whatever it was, it took her hard. I learned how to only need sleep every other night. Her last week of words didn’t make sense. Finally, I coaxed a creaking “I love you” out of her 8 days before I slammed the door on her neck. I just couldn’t help but wonder if she might have more life in her eyes if she just turned. She didn’t.

It has now been just me in our big and empty house. My home. I have reorganized the bookshelves 4 separate times and two consecutive times. I have made my room look like each cover of the home magazines we kept in the closet for years and years.

I haven’t gotten the trash out of the yard. I guess now it’s too late even to do one last lap. I know the yard would never be adorned with cushions torn by dogs or empty Tupperware containers turned to chew toys again. Call me sentimental. Call me a teenager in the apocalypse. I don’t care.

I curl up in my mother’s bed. The window to my left faces directly into the yard. I see a tree has knocked over the fence, and more stupid, idi otic dumb brains are ungracefully flooding the yard. I get under all three blankets and pull them up to my nose. I inhale my mother’s smell, wiltful and warm. Dog must underlaying it all.

I wake up to see darkness outside. There is an even more sinister shade from the yard, a sea of heads with a somewhat gravitational force pulling them toward my entry. I sit out of bed and make my way to the glass door. I rip the sheets from their nails and staples and stare at each face, knowing they only want to get in. I would like to see if I should let them.

An Ode to Cicadas in the Summer Heat

Piper Gross

The cicadas wait
For the sun to make their deafening call
Their voices build
Joined by others in the dappled leaves
Calls reverberate but haven’t crescendoed into one continuous melody
The cloud cover of summer thunderstorms kept them sleeping
And the humidity amplifies the resonance of their call Becoming all-encompassing
Only a cool breeze
From the understory of the surrounding forest breaks their spell

Solar Systems

Ivy Sauter-Sargent

The dancing girls and glamour boys surround the woman with moons in her ears. She sits alone at a table, her head hung with intoxication. The world around her sags down. Loose music earworms towards her. She glances up to catch a singer as glorious as a rising angel. All eyes weighed down with eyeliner and legs. The singer, silky, gorgeous, and clean stares at the woman. The woman is entrapped.

The moon woman waits to see if there will be an end and once her eyes are shut and there are no signs of the club stopping she leaves, unwillingly. She stops to look at the singer. The singer blinks slowly as if it were a wink. Her eyelashes seem to reach across the room and brush the woman’s cheeks.

The moon woman’s building seems to be crumbling towards the ground, its plaster cracking and hiding mice away in holes. As she makes her way up the stairs she pulls confetti out of her hair, abandoning it on the way up, leaving a trail of stars. She looks out her window seeing the ocean pushing at the shore, trying to find a foothold in the silken sand. The moon woman, not wanting to disturb the world around her, stumbles through the shadows. A breeze slips in from the ocean and catches her as she descends to her bed, a land of linen clouds and dreams.

The moon woman wakes with the singer plastered in her eyelids. She rolls her ankles, her neck, her wrists, trying to shake off the glorious eyes of the singer. Her coffee is bitter, she doesn’t speak as she goes through her day.

The glamour girls and dancing boys surround her again that night. She does not know their names, she does not ask, her eyes are glued to the stage. The singer appears again.

The moon woman waits. Watches that woman’s eyes as they blink at her. The singer leaves her post, coming down to the crowd. She swims through them. Her bright yellow dress, a sun in the night, flowing through the room towards the moon woman.

The singer leans down. Her lips brush the moon woman’s ear.

“You should not like my eyes.” Her voice is hot and burning against the moon woman.

The moon woman stares up, the singer’s eyes drip from her sockets like the sun through silk.

I fear

Andaluna Malki

I fear the day is here.
After greens and youth
reds and the wind’s colorful
whisperings. I feared their morphing.
I feared the drop. My window panes
of other worlds where colors were
the only existence.
Light became my favorite
humming as she pulled cascaded
through the branches stained glass
on my bed sheets.
And how they went so willingly
with the gusts from whatever has a chest so full and large.
And how I thought my panes will always be full.
The light will always hum.
Now it is gray. The light. The panes.
The wind rattles growing fingers
against the dark into my glass.
My room has risen into the fog.
My small lungs paint the glass,
matching the sky. I see deep
into the dry valley with
its crunch and brown carpet
where lightning bugs will hatch in the summer.

GIRL

Ruby Karyo

It never used to be that difficult; things were much easier, and I just failed to see it that way. Looking back, I shed tears. My soul cries. Those times when we used to walk, talk, and laugh like little kids, and we would say hi to everyone on the streets, and we would want to be that (particularity) Girl in the neighborhood who we thought had everything she’d ever wanted. We see it in our dreams and sometimes wonder if we’ll ever get there. Then, when we got there, ‘wishing,’ we never asked to be that Girl. The price we had to pay, the trauma she’d had to endure without us knowing the smiles on her cheekbones, was the silent death of her heart. And when she told her story, I realized all along she was me.

Dancing to "In My Room" by Frank Ocean

Kai Meyer

You’re lying on your bed, arms sprawling, pants off. Your chest is sinking into the pull of the mattress, feet still tethered to the floor. “In My Room” comes on.

A systematic beat. A little man knocking on the inside of a hollow heart, awakening the rust that has thickened.

A chin nod, a sway, a side to side, a body begins the slightest movements. The stress of a day held in the heel’s tapping, waxy resistance dripping off with each beat.

A small curl of anticipation, a fluttering within the nervous system. Ocean’s voice floods the speaker, “No sleeper seats, that’s a mattress. No sleeper seats, that’s a mattress,” the beat expanding. An electric ripple of sound forming in the air.

The slightest pause at the end of his words, bridging the electricity between you and computer currents, lifting your left arm. Left fingers be gin tapping and tracing the air with reflections of Ocean’s words. The song works its way through your pointer finger, down your forearm, around your elbow, as you begin reaching up and up.

“And I think you made for the life I lead,” holding your hand, as you slip blindly into the room. A safety net created in the four walls of your room.

A free dance of exhaustion turning to expansion, “Got this lust for life in me.” In the weight of your two feet, circular steps follow the flow of the beat, knees slightly bent, waiting for the joints to explode.

Neck rolling to quick lyrics, fingers darting from side to side, fol lowing Frank’s loose tongue, speaking to empty emotions of the day’s dis appointment. Letting it all wash through a drag of rhythmic release with looping synth lurking in the background.

“Quit being violent with me. Quit being violent with me. Quit be ing violent with me”

Two beats, frozen, holding your breath, waiting for the break, look ing down at bruised legs, “You make me violent.”

Sweeping limbs across the room, knees popping, shoulders rolling back, a day’s exhaustion reclaimed in the span of two minutes.

“My room, my room, my room with me.”

Shoulders swaying once more, head bobbing, feet tapping in a small revolution. Steps taken towards the rest of the day and a quiet peace between the eyes.

“I guess I can’t state my feelings too soon, I don’t know you. And I can’t put no threats in the air.”

Cuts off.

Does God make delicate things just to watch them get hurt?

Anna Sauerbrey

I am learning. You were born breathing
and that is the tragedy. Just before ten,
third and final, you slipped into the world
quiet and still. You were warm
when I found your body wet and bloody,
amniotic sac vacuum sealed to the fur
of your upper body. It surrounded your head,
thin and membranous, scarlet red veins
and pink capillaries from the concentration
of blood. Your placenta was on the ground
next to you, fluids seeping into the straw, a siren
song for predators just like you—
unmoving and iron rich.
